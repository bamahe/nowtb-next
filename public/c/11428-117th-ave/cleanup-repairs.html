The short version

The house is structurally sound and the bones are fine. The kitchen was updated at some point and it still shows well. What you are paying for is not repair so much as reset: getting other people's belongings out, getting the walls and ceilings back to a neutral surface, and getting the air conditioning serviced.

Four things drive the cost, in this order:

  • The clean-out. Mattresses, bikes, tarps, yard debris, and a shed that needs to be emptied.
  • The ceilings. Paint is peeling in sheets in several rooms, and the worst of it is directly around the air conditioning supply vents. That pattern points at the A/C, not the roof, but it needs a licensed eye before anyone paints over it.
  • Interior paint, wall to wall. The walls are heavily soiled at chair-rail height throughout. This is not a touch-up job.
  • A/C service. The space above the air handler, filter included, is packed with black debris, and the data plate reads 2007.

Everything past those four is detail.

Why the order matters

Paint peeling in a ring around supply registers is the classic signature of condensation, usually an uninsulated or leaking supply boot sweating into the ceiling in Florida humidity. It is normally a straightforward fix. But it can also be a roof leak, and the two look similar from below. I am not going to have anyone paint over this until an A/C contractor and a roofer have each put eyes on it. Painting first means paying twice and, worse, disclosing later.

I am also having the dark speckling assessed rather than assuming. It may be nothing more than surface mildew on failed paint, which is common and cheap to remediate. I would rather know, document it, and hand a buyer a resolved item than have it surface in their inspection.

Proposed: an A/C evaluation and a roof check before anything cosmetic, so we know the cause. Neither is booked. Both are diagnostic, and approving them does not commit you to a repair. The ceiling repair gets quoted once we know what we are fixing.

Two things to settle before anyone orders

The plank is ordered tight. 54 cases covers 1,306.8 sq ft against 1,253 sq ft of living area, a 4.3 percent overage. The trade norm for plank is 7 to 10 percent for cuts and waste, which would want 56 to 57 cases. That is only comfortable if the tiled baths and laundry are excluded from the scope. If the whole footprint is being floored, this is short, and running out mid-job means chasing a matching dye lot.

200 linear feet of quarter round may be light. A 1,253 sq ft house with this many finished spaces runs closer to 300 linear feet of wall once you total the rooms, even after taking out door openings and closets. Worth confirming against a measure rather than an estimate.

I owe you a straight word here, because this changes what I told you. Before I had a number, I said replacing the floor was not worth it and that closing the seams was enough. With the quote in front of me I would put it differently. This is a real option, and it is also the largest single line on this page.

The call comes down to how widespread the separation actually is. If it is a handful of seams in two rooms, closing them costs a few hundred dollars and the money does more work on paint and the clean-out. If the seams are opening throughout, buyers read the floor as worn no matter how clean the house is, and replacement earns its keep.

If you need to trim this budget anywhere, this is the first line I would cut, and I would not think less of the decision. It is the one item here that is genuinely optional.
