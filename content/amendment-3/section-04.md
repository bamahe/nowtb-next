# ## What Does Amendment 3 Do for Investors, Landlords, and Renters?

If you own Florida property you do not live in, Amendment 3 gives you one thing: the annual cap on assessment increases drops from 10% to 5%, for all levies other than school district levies, beginning January 1, 2027. You get no piece of the bigger homestead exemption. That is reserved for homesteaded primary residences.

The cap change is written into two separate places in Article VII, Section 4 of the Florida Constitution, and both read identically: "a. Before January 1, 2027, ten percent (10%). b. Beginning January 1, 2027, five percent (5%)."

| Subsection | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Art. VII s.4(g) | Residential real property, as defined by general law, containing nine units or fewer, that is not already under Save Our Homes |
| Art. VII s.4(h) | All other non-homestead real property: commercial, large multifamily, vacant land, second homes |

A duplex, a fourplex, a small apartment building, a snowbird condo, a commercial strip center, and a raw lot all land under the 5% cap. Different subsections, same number.

### What does the 5% cap not do?

**It does not touch school taxes.** For school district levies, non-homestead property stays completely uncapped and is assessed at just, or market, value every year. Current law's 10% cap already excluded school levies, and CS/HJR 1F preserves that exclusion.

**It caps value, not taxes.** The Manatee County Property Appraiser puts it plainly: the change "from 10% to 5% only caps assessed value, not taxes." Millage rates are set separately by each taxing authority and can rise. A capped assessment with a raised millage still produces a higher bill.

**It does nothing to non-ad valorem assessments.** CDD fees, fire, solid waste, and stormwater line items are not ad valorem taxes, so Amendment 3 does not reach them. They can still rise, or be newly adopted, no matter what happens on the ballot.

Nothing shows up until the August 2027 TRIM notice and the tax bill delivered in November 2027.

### Does the cost shift to renters?

The Revenue Estimating Conference projects local non-school property tax revenue falling by $4.95 billion in FY 2027 to 2028 and $8.78 billion in FY 2028 to 2029, with a recurring impact of $11.86 billion. Critics argue that hole gets filled somewhere, and that renters and non-homesteaded owners are the ones holding the bag.

The mechanisms opponents point to:

- **Fees instead of taxes.** Homebuilders warn the gap gets backfilled with impact fees, permit fees, mobility fees, and water and sewer connection fees, which raise the cost of new construction and get passed into the price of new homes and new rentals.
- **Affordable housing supply.** The Tax Foundation warns that income-restricted and affordable housing development "could decline as the capacity to raise rent is restricted, thereby reducing any return on investment."
- **No new homestead relief for rentals.** Every dollar of exemption growth goes to owner-occupants. A landlord's only benefit is slower assessed-value growth on the non-school portion of the bill.

### What do supporters say back?

Governor DeSantis has rebutted the renters critique directly, saying there is nothing in the amendment stating renters will pay more, calling the criticism "a fundamental misunderstanding of what it actually does," and pointing out that the measure lowers the assessment-growth cap on non-homesteaded property, the exact class most rentals fall into. Under current law a landlord absorbs up to 10% assessed growth a year. Beginning January 1, 2027, that ceiling is half as high, and in a fast-appreciating year that is real money that does not have to be passed through.

There is also a supply argument. Shelton Weeks, director of the Lucas Institute for Real Estate and Finance at Florida Gulf Coast University, says the tax relief could pull buyers into Florida rather than push them away, reversing recent outmigration driven by insurance and tax costs. Florida Realtors CEO Margy Grant has declined to predict a price spike, saying "The cost of home is economically driven. It's a supply and demand conversation, and when inventory increases, prices stabilize."

Both sides are forecasting. Neither has an actual tax bill in hand.

### Where does this leave you?

If you own a rental or a second home, run the math on the non-school portion of your bill only, assume your school portion keeps tracking market value, and assume your CDD and fire assessments do what they were always going to do. If you rent, the effect on your rent depends on your landlord's full cost stack, including insurance and fees, not on this one line in the constitution.

I can pull your current assessed value, just value, and millage breakdown for any Tampa Bay parcel if you want to see the actual numbers. For how the cap will be administered on your specific property, confirm with your county property appraiser, and take the tax treatment of any investment decision to your CPA. The ballot summary also requires the Legislature to prescribe a uniform procedure for counties and municipalities to raise the exemption further, and that procedure has to be written before anyone can use it.
