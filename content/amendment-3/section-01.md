# ## What Does Florida Amendment 3 Actually Do?

Amendment 3 does four separate things: it raises the homestead exemption that applies to **non-school** property taxes, it leaves the school portion of your bill alone, it cuts the annual assessment cap on non-homestead property from 10% to 5%, and it limits what counties and cities may spend property tax revenue on. It takes effect January 1, 2027 if at least 60% of voters approve it on November 3, 2026.

**Your Florida property tax bill has two buckets.** There is school district millage and there is everything else (county, municipal, and special district). Amendment 3 touches the "everything else" bucket almost exclusively. School taxes keep coming.

### How the homestead exemption changes

Today a Florida homestead gets $25,000 off all millage including schools, plus a second exemption of $26,411 that applies only to non-school millage, and only to assessed value above $50,000. That second number is the CPI-adjusted figure the Florida Department of Revenue certified for 2026. Total: $51,411, of which only $25,000 helps on school taxes.

Amendment 3 **replaces** that second band. It does not stack on top of it. The enrolled joint resolution strikes the old "greater than fifty thousand dollars and up to seventy-five thousand dollars" language and substitutes a new tiered non-school exemption. The result is $25,000 against school millage plus up to $150,000 or $250,000 against non-school millage, not $51,411 plus $150,000.

| | Current law (2026) | If Amendment 3 passes |
|---|---|---|
| Homestead exemption, school millage | $25,000 | $25,000, unchanged |
| Homestead exemption, non-school millage | $25,000 plus $26,411 (second band applies only above $50,000 of assessed value) | Up to $150,000 on Jan 1, 2027; up to $250,000 on Jan 1, 2028 |
| Inflation indexing | The $26,411 band is CPI-adjusted annually | The $250,000 tier is CPI-adjusted beginning Jan 1, 2029 |
| New residents (not a FL resident on Dec 31, 2026) | Same exemption as everyone else | $25,000 school plus $50,000 non-school, stepping up later; the $50,000 is CPI-adjusted beginning Jan 1, 2028 |
| Non-homestead assessment cap, non-school levies | 10% per year | 5% per year beginning Jan 1, 2027 |
| Non-homestead assessment cap, school levies | No cap, assessed at just value | No cap, unchanged |
| Save Our Homes cap on homesteads | Lower of 3% or CPI | Unchanged |
| Portability of the Save Our Homes differential | $500,000 max, three-year lookback | Unchanged |

### What that is worth in dollars

Against non-school millage only, the net new exempt value is about **$98,589 in 2027** ($150,000 minus the $51,411 you already have) and roughly **$198,589 in 2028**. The Pinellas County Property Appraiser ran those numbers against the 2025 countywide aggregate average non-school rate of 12.2011 mills and estimated savings of approximately **$1,203 in 2027 and $2,423 in 2028** for a homestead large enough to use the full exemption.

Two caveats on that math. The 2028 figure assumes the $26,411 tier is frozen at its 2026 value; it is CPI-indexed, so the real 2028 counterfactual delta is closer to $197,000 to $198,000. And against school millage the change is **zero**. Your savings depend on your own assessed value and your own taxing authorities' non-school millage. Run your own numbers with your county property appraiser.

### The new-resident rule

If you did not maintain a permanent residence in Florida as of December 31, 2026, you get a smaller version: $25,000 against school levies and up to $50,000 against non-school levies. Then you step up to the full amount.

The ballot and the joint resolution do not describe the same clock. The summary printed on the ballot says the increased exemption begins "with the fifth year of exemption, to the extent permitted by the U.S. Constitution," and the constitutional text matches it: "beginning with the fifth year of exemption under this subparagraph." The ballot summary written into the enrolled CS/HJR 1F says something different, requiring a person who establishes Florida residency after January 1, 2027 to "maintain Florida residency for five years prior to receiving the increased homestead exemption." Those are not the same clock. Years of exemption start when you qualify for homestead. Residency starts when you move. The Pinellas appraiser reads it as the exemption clock, writing that after maintaining a Florida homestead exemption for four years the owner becomes eligible for the larger exemption on January 1 of the fifth year. That conflict is live and is the subject of the ballot-language litigation. The step-up amount is also the CPI-adjusted figure from 2029 forward, not a frozen $250,000.

Two more details. The ballot summary hedges the entire provision with "to the extent permitted by the U.S. Constitution." And beginning January 1, 2030, a county or municipality may shorten the five-year wait by a two-thirds vote of its governing body for a "critical local need."

### The 5% non-homestead cap

Amendment 3 cuts the annual assessment-increase cap on non-homestead property from 10% to 5%, effective January 1, 2027. The change is written into two places in Article VII, Section 4: subsection (g), covering residential property of nine units or fewer not already under Save Our Homes, and subsection (h), covering all other non-homestead real property. Both open with "For all levies other than school district levies."

Rentals, second homes, land, and commercial property get a tighter cap on the county and city side, and remain completely uncapped for school millage, assessed at just value every year. The Manatee County Property Appraiser puts the key limitation plainly: going from 10% to 5% "only caps assessed value, not taxes." Millage rates are set separately by each taxing authority and can rise. A capped assessment is not a capped bill.

### What does not change

Save Our Homes survives intact. Homestead assessment increases stay capped at the lower of 3% or the CPI change. Portability stays at a $500,000 maximum transferable differential with the three-year lookback.

One warning about secondhand summaries: the House staff analysis contains a line saying the new exemption "would apply to all ad valorem taxes." The constitutional text and the ballot summary both contradict that. Trust the text.

### The spending restriction and the local-option path

A new Article VII, Section 9(a)(2) limits county and municipal ad valorem revenue to seven enumerated uses: public safety, education and schools, infrastructure, natural resources, bond debt service, employee retirement benefits, and operations and administration. Other expenditures may be approved by county officers or governing bodies unless prohibited by general law, which makes the last category a broad catch-all.

The amendment also requires the Legislature to prescribe a uniform procedure letting counties and municipalities raise the homestead exemption **up to full assessed value**, and lets special districts do the same subject to referendum, no more than once in a 12-month period, with optional CPI indexing. There is no automatic elimination schedule in the text. The "schedule for full elimination" language from the original draft summary is gone. What remains is a pathway local governments could choose to use without another statewide vote.

### When you would actually see it

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| November 3, 2026 | Vote. Needs 60% approval |
| January 1, 2027 | Amendment takes effect; $150,000 non-school exemption and 5% cap apply |
| August 2027 | First TRIM notice reflecting the change |
| November 2027 | First tax bill reflecting the change |
| January 1, 2028 | Exemption rises to $250,000; new-resident $50,000 tier begins CPI indexing |
| January 1, 2029 | $250,000 tier begins CPI indexing |
| January 1, 2030 | Local governments may shorten the new-resident wait by two-thirds vote |

On the other side of the ledger, the Revenue Estimating Conference projects local non-school property tax revenue impacts of negative $4.95 billion in FY 2027-28, negative $8.78 billion in FY 2028-29, and a recurring impact of negative $11.86 billion. No financial impact statement appears on the ballot, because legislatively referred amendments are not required to carry one.
